Spring Garden

A cheerful collection of flowers blooming in the fresh colors of the spring season across East and South India.

Spring, known as Basanta in Bengal and Odisha and Vasantha in the South, is one of the most colorful seasons of the Indian year. Arriving between February and April, it softens the last chill of winter with warmer days, longer sunshine, and a gentle, scented breeze. Trees that stood bare through the cool months suddenly burst into bloom, and streets, parks, and village lanes glow with orange, red, pink, lavender, and gold.

Spring is deeply tied to the cultural life of East and South India. In Bengal and Odisha the season is marked by Basanta Panchami, Dol Jatra, and Holi, followed by the new year festivals of mid-April. In the South, Ugadi, Vishu, and Puthandu bring the new year with them. From the Palash-covered countryside of Bengal to the Jacaranda-lined avenues of Bengaluru and the golden Kanikonna of Kerala, the spring flowers of these regions carry both beauty and tradition. 🌸🌼

Palash

Palash (Butea monosperma), also known as Flame of the Forest, is the flower most closely linked with the arrival of spring in East India. Between February and April its bare branches fill with clusters of vivid orange-red blooms with curved, beak-like petals, and whole stretches of countryside seem to glow like fire. It grows across West Bengal, Odisha, Jharkhand, and Bihar, and it is the state flower of Jharkhand.

Palash is woven into the spring culture of the East. The poems and songs of Rabindranath Tagore made it a symbol of Bengali spring, and it is an essential part of the spring celebrations at Santiniketan. Its petals are also used to make natural orange and yellow colors for Dol Jatra and Holi, and the historic town of Palashi (Plassey) is named after the tree. A hardy, sun-loving tree that copes well with dry soil, it suits large gardens and open grounds best.

Madhabilata

Madhabilata (Hiptage benghalensis), also called Madhavi or the Helicopter Flower, is a graceful climber that perfumes the gardens of Bengal and the wider East in spring. Its loose clusters of strongly fragrant flowers have fringed white petals with a soft pink blush, and one petal carries a golden-yellow blotch at its center. After the blooms fade, the plant forms unusual three-winged fruits that spin through the air like tiny helicopters.

Madhabilata is loved in Bengali literature for its sweet scent, and one of its traditional names, Vasantduti, means “messenger of spring.” It grows fast and needs a strong support such as an arch, pergola, or fence, along with plenty of sunlight for a generous bloom. With regular trimming it can also be shaped into a bush or grown in a large container.

Shimul

Shimul (Bombax ceiba), the Red Silk Cotton tree, greets the early days of spring in Bengal with a dramatic show of crimson. From late winter into early spring, usually between January and March, the tall, thorny-trunked tree stands almost leafless while its branches fill with large, cup-shaped, deep red flowers. The blossoms are rich in nectar and attract a busy crowd of birds and insects, so a flowering Shimul is as lively as it is beautiful.

Later in spring, its pods split open and release soft white fibers that drift through the air like snowfall, a familiar sight across eastern India. This silky cotton has traditionally been used to stuff pillows and cushions. Shimul grows into a very tall tree, so it is best suited to parks, roadsides, and open grounds rather than small gardens.

Jacaranda

Jacaranda (Jacaranda mimosifolia) paints the avenues of South India in soft shades of lavender and violet. In Bengaluru, its clusters of bell-shaped flowers open in March and April against delicate, fern-like leaves, and the fallen blooms often carpet roads and pavements in purple. Along with the pink Tabebuia, it is one of the signature sights of the city's spring.

Native to South America, Jacaranda has adapted so well to the mild, dry spring of the Deccan plateau that it now feels like a part of the local landscape. It grows best in full sun and well-drained soil, and it needs plenty of space for its wide, airy crown, which makes it a tree for avenues, parks, and large gardens.

Tabebuia

Tabebuia (Tabebuia rosea), widely known as the Pink Trumpet Tree, turns South Indian streets into a soft pink dream in early spring. In Bengaluru the trees stand almost leafless while their branches fill with papery, trumpet-shaped flowers in shades of pink from blush to rose. The display is so striking that residents often compare it to cherry blossom season.

The flowers appear mainly between February and April, and a tree in full bloom showers petals that scatter like confetti beneath it. Although Tabebuia comes from Central and South America, it has become naturalized in Bengaluru and other cities of the South, where it is planted along roads and in parks and gardens. It loves full sun and warm, dry weather.

Kanikonna

Kanikonna (Cassia fistula), the Golden Shower tree, is the state flower of Kerala and one of the brightest sights of South India's spring. Its long, hanging clusters of golden-yellow flowers sway from the branches like chandeliers, and in full bloom the tree is almost leafless. The flowering season stretches from spring into early summer, with the display at its best around mid-April.

Kanikonna is inseparable from Vishu, the Malayalam New Year. Its flowers are an essential part of the Vishukkani, the auspicious arrangement of flowers, fruits, and a lit lamp that is the first sight of the new year, symbolizing prosperity. Across the country it goes by many names, including Sonalu in Bengal, Sunari in Odisha, Amaltas in Hindi, and Kakke in Kannada. It is drought tolerant and needs little care once established.
